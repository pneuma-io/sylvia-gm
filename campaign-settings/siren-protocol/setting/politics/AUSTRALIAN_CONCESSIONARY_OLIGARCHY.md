# Australian Concessionary Oligarchy

**Status:** creator-approved setting canon  
**Present year:** 2225  
**Scope:** Australia, especially the Sydney coastal showcase region

## Core model

Australia in 2225 is governed through a **patchwork of surviving public government and privately controlled jurisdiction**.

The corporate oligarchy did not arrive through one constitutional rupture, coup, revolution, or formal abolition of democracy. Public power was surrendered through a long sequence of individually defensible concessions that accumulated for more than a century.

A government might begin by allowing a company to privately own and operate one airport section of a high-speed rail corridor. Two decades later, the concession is extended because the private operator already owns the rolling stock, maintenance systems, stations, power contracts, ticketing identity layer, and surrounding development rights. Ten years later the extension grows again. After a century, one corporate bloc can effectively control a trunk transport system linking Sydney with Brisbane and Melbourne even though public governments still exist on paper and continue to regulate other parts of the transport network.

The same pattern occurred across energy, compute, AI infrastructure, telecommunications, housing, ports, logistics, medicine, reproductive services, finance, security, water, transport, and other essential systems.

## How public power eroded

The erosion of public authority was incremental rather than dramatic.

Typical concessions included:

- selling or long-leasing public land;
- granting exclusive operating rights;
- outsourcing maintenance and service delivery;
- allowing private security on privately controlled infrastructure;
- giving infrastructure owners limited rule-making authority inside their property;
- letting companies build private extensions to public systems;
- permitting vertically integrated ownership of infrastructure, services, data, identity, and payment systems;
- creating public-private arrangements that became practically impossible to unwind;
- accepting corporate financing in exchange for control rights;
- relying on private AI and compute capacity to operate public administration;
- allowing emergency or temporary arrangements to become permanent;
- repeatedly expanding existing concessions because rebuilding public alternatives was more expensive than renewing them.

Every individual decision could be defended as pragmatic, temporary, fiscally responsible, technologically necessary, or too small to threaten public sovereignty. The cumulative result was the opposite.

## Political culture during the transition

The setting's political satire extrapolates institutional complacency, corruption, lobbying, revolving-door employment, media outrage cycles, and short electoral incentives into a two-century failure of collective restraint.

Political institutions repeatedly reacted to each new concession with public controversy, hearings, warnings, inquiries, election promises, and moral panic while still allowing the overall transfer of power to continue.

Many office-holders came to treat politics primarily as a stable career: preserve salary, status, party position, donor relationships, future board appointments, consulting work, or quiet side arrangements; avoid crises large enough to end a career; and leave the difficult structural fight to a later government.

Some politicians and public servants genuinely resisted corporate capture. Some administrations reversed individual concessions. Some jurisdictions retained stronger public systems than others. The process was therefore contested rather than universally corrupt.

The long-term trend nevertheless favoured corporate ownership because the oligarchies controlled the energy, AI/compute, networks, capital, employment, and infrastructure increasingly required for government itself to function.

## Law versus effective control

Formal legal sovereignty and practical power are different layers.

A public government may retain constitutional authority over a district while a corporation owns the railway, station, surrounding towers, network access, energy contract, identity gates, private security, and payment systems through which the district actually functions.

Elsewhere, a corporation may possess explicit territorial or functional jurisdiction through long-term charter, concession, lease, development compact, special economic zone, infrastructure covenant, or similar legal instrument.

Sydney therefore contains both:

- **public territory dominated indirectly by corporate dependency**, and
- **territory or infrastructure where companies possess explicit private jurisdictional powers**.

This is the canonical meaning of the Sydney region being a **patchwork of both**.

## The infrastructure ratchet

Privatisation tends to become self-reinforcing.

Once a company owns one part of a system, it gains strong arguments for controlling adjacent parts: compatibility, security, maintenance efficiency, financing, data integration, safety, scheduling, and customer convenience.

Each extension increases the cost of replacing the company and makes the next extension easier to justify.

After several generations, residents may depend on a corporation for transport, housing, network identity, work credentials, insurance, security, energy billing, and access to services without ever having consciously chosen to live under a corporate government.

This **infrastructure ratchet** is one of the defining mechanisms of Australian corporate power.

## Why government still matters

Public government is diminished, not fictional.

It still runs courts, legislates, taxes, conducts elections, maintains public agencies, negotiates treaties, regulates activity outside private concessions, delivers services, owns some infrastructure, and can sometimes use competition law, public procurement, courts, taxation, or popular legitimacy against corporate interests.

Governments also remain one of the few institutions capable of coordinating across competing corporate systems.

The corporate oligarchies therefore spend enormous resources influencing, capturing, bargaining with, litigating against, funding, undermining, and occasionally cooperating with public government rather than simply abolishing it.

## The six principal oligarchies

Australia's dominant corporate blocs are overlapping conglomerates with different **primary spines**, not six clean monopolies.

- **INLAND Systems Group (`INLAND`)** grew from desert solar, storage, transmission, hyperscale compute, and AI infrastructure.
- **Meridian Transit & Development (`MERIDIAN`)** grew from transport concessions, freight, stations, ports, and transport-led property development; it now controls the principal Sydney-Brisbane-Melbourne high-speed trunk.
- **Morrow Genomics & Health (`MORROW`)** grew from hospitals, reproductive medicine, genetic infrastructure, cybernetics, and pharmaceuticals.
- **Lattice Civic Systems (`LATTICE`)** grew from networks, finance, payments, insurance, identity, and credential infrastructure.
- **Palisade Defence & Security (`PALISADE`)** grew from private security, defence systems, drones, intelligence, and critical-infrastructure protection.
- **Bluehaven Urban Systems (`BLUEHAVEN`)** grew from water, housing, property, urban services, utilities, and coastal resilience.

Their full faction profiles are in [Australian Corporate Oligarchies](../factions/AUSTRALIAN_OLIGARCHIES.md).

## Why corporations do not form one state

The major oligarchies compete and depend on one another.

Energy ownership does not automatically equal transport ownership. Compute dominance does not automatically equal medical dominance. A security bloc may need access to another company's network; a transport company may depend on an energy supplier; a reproductive-health conglomerate may depend on public recognition of its records.

Cross-holdings further complicate the picture. LATTICE can finance a rival, INLAND can power it, PALISADE can secure it, MERIDIAN can move its workforce, BLUEHAVEN can house them, and MORROW can operate the hospitals they use. Public pension funds and government investment vehicles may hold shares in companies that public agencies also regulate.

This mutual dependency prevents clean monopoly and keeps the political landscape unstable.

Smaller actors exploit those seams: unions, professional bodies, municipal governments, Aboriginal organisations and land interests, cooperatives, neighbourhood associations, advocacy groups, syndicates, gangs, hacker collectives, journalists, investigators, wealthy individuals, and player characters can matter because the large powers are divided.

## Sydney application

The Sydney coastal megaregion should be mapped as a mosaic of different authority arrangements rather than clean national/corporate borders.

A traveller may pass through, in sequence:

1. an ordinary publicly governed municipal district;
2. a MERIDIAN railway station under a century-old corporate transport concession;
3. a privately owned train where corporate security and contract rules apply;
4. a mixed-use development built above the line under a separate corporate charter;
5. a public interchange policed by a regional authority;
6. a neighbourhood where a syndicate or gang has stronger street control than either company or government;
7. a community-controlled or cooperative precinct with its own local institutions.

Crossing a property boundary, station gate, bridge, tunnel, network boundary, or service contract can therefore change who has practical authority even when the traveller has not crossed a traditional political border.

The six oligarchies are unevenly entrenched across the region rather than separated by clean territorial borders. Their concentration of influence follows the infrastructure they own: energy and compute, transport, medicine, identity/finance, security, housing/water/urban services.

## Design rule

Do not portray Australian corporate power as having replaced politics with one omnipotent company.

The setting's distinctive political texture comes from **two centuries of accumulated concessions, institutional dependency, corruption, self-interest, genuine public resistance, competing corporate ownership, cross-holdings, and overlapping law**.

The result is messy, legalistic, hypocritical, functional, corruptible, occasionally reformable, and permanently contested.

The six principal oligarchies must also remain meaningfully useful institutions. They provide real energy, transport, healthcare, interoperability, security, housing, water, and urban services that people genuinely value. Their danger comes from structural dependency and concentrated power, not from every employee or service being malicious.
